## The question this lesson answers

Stable angina is confirmed: which drug now, which next, which never together, and what protects the heart?

- **The drug ladder (17).** First line a beta-blocker or a CCB, selected for the patient (active asthma → a suitable CCB)
  (5). Titrate to the highest tolerated dose before adding (2). Still symptomatic on a beta-blocker → add a
  dihydropyridine (amlodipine, nifedipine MR), never verapamil or diltiazem (6). On verapamil, no beta-blocker →
  nicorandil (1). Taking sildenafil → nifedipine MR, no nitrate or nicorandil (2). Vasospastic angina → CCB, nitrate (2).
- **The other drugs, one fact each (9).** Nitrates raise cGMP; nitrate tolerance → asymmetric ISMN (3); nicorandil is a
  potassium-channel activator, ulcers anywhere in the gut (3); ivabradine causes phosphenes (1); stop nicorandil before
  sildenafil (1).
- **Prognosis (4).** Antiplatelet treatment, statin and blood pressure treatment; consider an ACE inhibitor with
  diabetes. Other indications for ACE inhibitors belong to their own lessons.
- **Stents (2).** The prescribed antiplatelet course matters; premature interruption increases stent thrombosis risk.
  Avoid the outdated blanket claim that every drug-eluting stent needs a longer course than every bare-metal stent.

## Concepts, explained

**Two useful groups (from lesson 2's supply and demand).** These organise the main mechanisms, not every drug or
every permissible combination. Vasodilation also lowers demand; it does not remove a fixed plaque.
- **Slow the heart** (lower demand): beta-blocker, rate-limiting CCB (verapamil, diltiazem), ivabradine.
- **Open the vessels** (lower the load, widen the supply): dihydropyridine CCB (amlodipine, felodipine, nifedipine MR),
  nitrates, nicorandil.
The specific combination to teach is beta-blocker + a dihydropyridine CCB. Avoid beta-blocker + verapamil or diltiazem
for routine angina treatment: bradycardia, AV block and worsening heart failure. Do not teach "never combine two
rate-lowering drugs": ivabradine can be added to a beta-blocker in eligible patients. Ranolazine sits outside these
two groups. Keep the exceptions on reveal, so the opening remains simple.

**Relief now: GTN (CG126 1.3.3–1.3.4).** Spray or tablet under the tongue, for pain and before planned exertion.
Pain still there after 5 minutes → a second dose; still there 5 minutes after that → call an ambulance (it may be an
MI). Side effects: headache, flushing, light-headedness (sit down first). How it works: nitric oxide → cGMP → veins relax
→ less blood returns → less work (lower preload). Never with sildenafil or other PDE5 inhibitors: both raise cGMP and
the pressure collapses.

**The first drug (1.4.5–1.4.8).** A beta-blocker or a CCB; neither is universally preferred. Choose by pulse, blood
pressure, comorbidities, contraindications and preference. Active asthma makes a CCB useful; verapamil/diltiazem
require a suitable pulse and no HFrEF or conduction disease. A dihydropyridine is also a first-line CCB option.
Review after 2–4 weeks and titrate as tolerated. Example: bisoprolol 2.5 mg, pulse 78, adequate BP, no adverse effects
→ increase before adding. The hook's pulse 52 limits further titration even though the dose is below the licensed maximum.

**The second drug (1.4.9).** Still pain at the highest tolerated dose → switch to the other, or combine. Beta-blocker +
**dihydropyridine** CCB (amlodipine, felodipine, nifedipine MR). Never beta-blocker + verapamil or diltiazem.
Amlodipine's own side effects: ankle swelling, flushing, headache.

**When first-line options cannot be used (1.4.11–1.4.12).** If neither beta-blocker nor CCB is suitable, consider
long-acting nitrate, ivabradine, nicorandil or ranolazine monotherapy. If one first-line drug is tolerated but insufficient
and the other is unsuitable, consider an eligible add-on from this list. These are not routine first-line choices
(1.4.10). Analogy: two keys first; these are the spare keys.
- **Isosorbide mononitrate**: tolerance if the level never falls. Asymmetric dosing leaves a nitrate-low night.
  Use **08:00 and 16:00** for the specific immediate-release product cited above (eight hours apart).
  Once-daily MR regimens depend on the formulation.
- **Nicorandil**: a potassium-channel opener with a nitrate part. Ulcers anywhere from mouth to anus, and skin and eye;
  stop it if they appear. Second-line only (MHRA 2016). Not with PDE5 inhibitors.
- **Ivabradine**: blocks the If ("funny") current in the sinus node, slowing the rate only. Needs sinus rhythm and a
  resting rate of 70 or more. Phosphenes (flashes of light). Not with verapamil, diltiazem or strong CYP3A4 inhibitors
  such as clarithromycin (MHRA 2014).
- **Ranolazine**: blocks the late sodium current; prolongs the QT. Named only.

**Two drugs and still pain (1.4.13–1.4.14, 1.5.1–1.5.2).** With persistent symptoms despite optimal treatment, offer
angiography to guide possible PCI/CABG. A third symptom drug is reserved for waiting for revascularisation or when
it is unsuitable or declined. Controlled on two → no routine third drug. High-risk anatomy can warrant intervention
even with controlled symptoms; detailed prognostic indications are outside this lesson.

**Stents.** After elective PCI for stable atherosclerotic disease, aspirin plus clopidogrel is the usual DAPT pair.
Six months is the default with low bleeding risk and no oral anticoagulation indication; selected patients have
shorter courses (1–3 months), especially with high bleeding risk. Then a long-term single antiplatelet, aspirin or
clopidogrel, as prescribed. Unplanned early interruption raises stent thrombosis risk; this may present as STEMI.
The lesson teaches adherence to the prescribed course, not a universal duration or unsupervised stopping rule.

**Protect the heart (1.3.5–1.3.8, NG238).** Separate symptom relief from event prevention in established atherosclerotic
CAD. Do not claim beta-blockers never improve prognosis: MI/HFrEF are separate indications.
- **Aspirin 75 mg**, considering bleeding risk and comorbidities (clopidogrel is an alternative where appropriate).
- **Atorvastatin 80 mg**, irrespective of baseline cholesterol; a lower dose may be appropriate for interactions,
  adverse-effect risk or preference.
- **Blood pressure** treated.
- **Consider an ACE inhibitor** with diabetes; assess BP, renal function, potassium and contraindications.
Plus stopping smoking. Analogy: symptom drugs ease the traffic; prevention drugs reduce future blockage risk.

**Angina with clear arteries (from lesson 2).**
- **Vasospastic**: a CCB (amlodipine, diltiazem) first; add a long-acting nitrate if needed. Avoid beta-blockers for
  isolated vasospasm (particularly non-selective ones);
  stop smoking and cocaine, avoid triptans.
- **Microvascular**: tailor symptom therapy to mechanism; beta-blocker/CCB may suit impaired flow reserve, while
  microvascular spasm favours a CCB. Treat risk factors; do not automatically prescribe antiplatelets just because
  there is angina with no obstructive CAD. Detailed coronary function testing is outside scope.

## Pitfalls and exam tricks

- "On a beta-blocker, still angina": check the dose first. Low dose, pulse 78 → increase it. Highest tolerated dose, pulse
  in the 50s → add amlodipine or nifedipine MR.
- Verapamil + beta-blocker is the classic wrong option.
- Active asthma → a suitable CCB; verapamil/diltiazem only with suitable pulse, LV function and conduction.
- Sildenafil → no nitrates, no nicorandil; add a dihydropyridine.
- Fading ISMN benefit with evenly spaced doses and an unchanged stable pattern → consider tolerance and correct
  the schedule. New/worsening symptoms or rest pain require reassessment, not an assumed tolerance diagnosis.
- Mouth or anal ulcers → nicorandil. Flashes of light → ivabradine.
- "Reduces the risk of MI" in uncomplicated stable atherosclerotic angina → aspirin/statin, rather than a symptom drug.
- Two drugs, controlled → no third. Two drugs, not controlled → angiography.
- Vasospasm → CCB, not a beta-blocker.
- STEMI weeks after a stent → was the clopidogrel stopped?

## Numbers (the same man as lesson 2)

- Patient A, the man of 61 with diabetes, hypertension and smoking from lesson 2. CTCA now shows 70% proximal LAD stenosis.
- Started on GTN, aspirin 75 mg, atorvastatin 80 mg, ramipril (diabetes), and bisoprolol, raised to 10 mg last month.
- Today: the same brief exertional episodes twice a week, no rest pain or recent worsening; sinus pulse 52,
  blood pressure 132/80, preserved LV function. No CCB contraindication.
- Hook question: "Which drug next?" **No options on the Learn hook.** c01 options shuffled: Amlodipine (answer), Verapamil, Increase bisoprolol, Isosorbide
  mononitrate.
- Why: pulse 52 leaves no room to raise the beta-blocker (its licensed maximum is 20 mg; his rate is the limit);
  verapamil with a beta-blocker is dangerous; a long-acting nitrate is for when a CCB cannot be used.
