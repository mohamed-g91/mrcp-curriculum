## The question this lesson answers

A patient with chest pain: is it angina, and which test does NICE want first?

## What the bank asks (8 questions, plus the Pivot batch)

- New exertional chest pain, no known disease → CT coronary angiography (5, one in a diabetic); it is also the most
  accurate non-invasive test for coronary disease (1).
- Exercise test: a fall in blood pressure is the strongest reason to stop (1).
- Syndrome X (microvascular angina): angina, ST depression on stress testing, normal angiogram (3).
- The Pivot batch adds: known disease → functional imaging; non-anginal pain + ST-T changes or Q waves → CTCA; CTCA
  non-diagnostic → functional imaging; asthma → dobutamine, not adenosine.

## Concepts, explained

**Supply and demand (why exertion).** A narrowed artery still carries enough blood at rest. Climbing a hill, the heart
beats faster and harder and needs more; the narrowing cannot deliver it, the muscle beyond runs short, and it hurts.
Stop, the demand falls, the pain goes within minutes. Analogy: a road narrowed to one lane is fine at 3 a.m. and jams at
rush hour. This is where the three features come from: squeezing pain, brought on by effort, gone with rest.

**The three features (1.3.3.1).** Anginal pain is:
1. constricting discomfort in the front of the chest, or in the neck, shoulders, jaw or arms;
2. brought on by physical exertion;
3. relieved by rest or GTN within about 5 minutes.
**Three = typical angina. Two = atypical angina. One or none = non-anginal chest pain.**
The same in men and women and in every ethnic group (1.3.3.2–3).
Real examples: "Tight across my chest walking to the bus, gone a couple of minutes after I stop" = 3, typical.
"Tight on the hill, but it takes 20 minutes to go" = 2, atypical. "A sharp stab under the left breast, at rest, worse
when I breathe in" = 0, non-anginal.

**What makes angina unlikely (1.3.3.5).** Pain that is continuous or very prolonged; unrelated to activity; brought on by
breathing in; or comes with dizziness, palpitations, tingling or difficulty swallowing. Think of the gut or the chest
wall instead.

**What makes it more likely (1.3.3.4).** Age, male sex, smoking, diabetes, hypertension, high cholesterol, a family
history of early coronary disease, other vascular disease, known coronary disease. These raise suspicion; they do not
change the typical / atypical / non-anginal label. (Since 2016 NICE no longer uses pre-test probability tables to pick
the test.)

**What to do with each label.**
- **Non-anginal**: angina is excluded (1.3.1.1), and no cardiac test is offered (1.3.3.12), **unless** the resting ECG
  shows ST-T changes or Q waves, or something else in the history raises suspicion.
- **Typical or atypical**: offer testing (1.3.1.2).

**Everyone being investigated (1.3.2.2, 1.3.3.7–1.3.3.13).**
- Examine for other causes of angina: aortic stenosis, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy.
- Bloods for what makes angina worse, such as **anaemia** (a full blood count).
- A **resting 12-lead ECG**. A normal ECG does **not** rule angina out (1.3.3.11). Changes that point to coronary
  disease: pathological Q waves, LBBB, ST and T wave changes (1.3.3.13).
- Chest X-ray only if another diagnosis (a lung tumour) is suspected.

**Which test? (1.3.4.3–1.3.5.2.)**
- **No known disease**, typical or atypical angina (or non-anginal with ST-T changes or Q waves) →
  **CT coronary angiography** (64-slice or above). It photographs the arteries.
- **Known disease** (old MI, a stent or bypass, a previous angiogram) → a **functional test** (an exercise ECG may be
  used instead). The question is no longer "is there disease?" but "is this narrowing causing the pain?" (1.3.4.4).
- CTCA shows disease of **uncertain significance**, or is non-diagnostic → **functional imaging** (1.3.5.1).
- Functional imaging **inconclusive** → **invasive coronary angiography**, third line (1.3.5.2).
Analogy: CTCA is a photograph of the road; a functional test is the traffic report; invasive angiography is driving down
it with a camera.

**Reading the result (box 1, 1.3.7.1–3).**
- Significant disease on CTCA or invasive angiography = a narrowing of **70% or more** in a major artery, or **50% or
  more in the left main**. Or reversible ischaemia on functional imaging. Either confirms stable angina (lesson 3).
- Smaller narrowings can still cause angina when something else adds to it: anaemia, spasm, a fast heart, LVH.
- No significant disease → look for other causes: HCM, **syndrome X**, or a non-cardiac cause.

**The functional tests and how they stress the heart (1.3.6.1–3).**
| Test | What it sees | Stress |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Myocardial perfusion scan (SPECT) | Blood reaching the muscle | Adenosine, dipyridamole or dobutamine |
| Stress echocardiogram | A wall that stops moving well | Exercise or dobutamine |
| MR perfusion (first-pass, contrast) | Blood reaching the muscle | Adenosine or dipyridamole |
| MR wall motion | A wall that stops moving well | Exercise or dobutamine |
Exam trick: adenosine and dipyridamole can cause bronchospasm, so **asthma → dobutamine** (stress echo, or SPECT with
dobutamine). Dobutamine does what exercise does (faster, harder heart); adenosine instead widens healthy arteries and
shows the narrowed one by contrast.

**The exercise ECG.** Not used to diagnose or exclude angina in people without known disease (1.3.6.5): it misses too
much. It keeps one place: known disease, where it may be used instead of functional imaging (1.3.4.4). When to stop
the test is left out (one bank question; NICE does not cover it).

**Angina with clear arteries (1.3.7.3).**
- **Microvascular angina (cardiac syndrome X)**: typical exertional angina, ST depression on stress testing, but the
  large arteries are clear; the fault is in the tiny vessels. More often women. Analogy: the motorway is clear, the
  side streets are jammed.
- **Vasospastic (Prinzmetal) angina**: the artery clamps itself shut. Pain at **rest**, often early morning, with
  transient **ST elevation**; normal angiogram; smoking, cocaine, triptans. (Its treatment is lesson 3.)
- **Non-coronary angina**: aortic stenosis, HCM (examine; echo).

## Pitfalls and exam tricks

- Count the features; relief takes "about 5 minutes": pain that lasts 20 minutes after stopping loses the third feature.
- Non-anginal pain needs no test, unless the resting ECG shows ST-T changes or Q waves.
- A normal resting ECG never rules angina out.
- New angina, no known disease → CTCA. Not an exercise ECG, not a stress echo, not invasive angiography.
- Known disease (stent, MI, bypass) → functional test, not CTCA.
- CTCA uncertain → functional imaging → still unclear → invasive angiography. In that order.
- Asthma → dobutamine, never adenosine or dipyridamole.
- Exertional angina, ST depression on stress, clear arteries → syndrome X. Rest pain at dawn with ST elevation, clear
  arteries → vasospasm.
- Angina with an ejection systolic murmur → echo first (aortic stenosis).
- Response to GTN is not used to diagnose an ACS (CG95 1.2.1.4); in stable pain it is part of feature 3.

  discomfort; not brought on by exertion, not relieved within 5 minutes: non-anginal, but an abnormal ECG.)
